The Emergency Incarcerations and the Primacy of Natural Liberty

When executive emergency decrees purport to suspend habeas corpus, the Supreme Court's majority ruling yields to an immortal dissent vindicated by constitutional history.

Key takeaways

  • The right to life and personal liberty under Article 21 is permanent and can never be suspended under modern Indian constitutional law.
  • Habeas corpus represents the cornerstone of judicial protection against arbitrary administrative confinement.
  • A judicial dissent rooted in constitutional principles can appeal to the intelligence of a future day and eventually become governing law.
  • The Forty-Fourth Amendment established decisive structural barriers ensuring that executive emergency powers cannot trample fundamental freedoms.

The case in brief

In June 1975, the President of India issued a proclamation of internal emergency, followed two days later by an extraordinary presidential order under Article 359(1) suspending the right of any person to move any court for the enforcement of fundamental rights under Articles 14, 21, and 22. Armed with preventive detention legislation, state authorities arrested thousands of political dissenters, journalists, and citizens across the nation without specifying charges. When detainees approached various high courts seeking writs of habeas corpus, the executive raised a threshold preliminary objection asserting that judicial inquiry into the legality of detentions was entirely extinguished.

Remarkably, nine separate high courts rejected the executive's total immunity argument, holding that despite the emergency order, courts retained the constitutional authority under Article 226 to review whether a detention was lawful, authorized by statute, or tainted by mala fides. The state appealed to the Supreme Court of India, where the Attorney General argued before a five-judge Constitution Bench that even if an executive officer acted out of personal malice or shot a citizen without authority of law, the courts were completely powerless to grant relief.

By a four-to-one majority, the Supreme Court accepted the executive's sweeping contention, ruling that Article 21 was the sole repository of the right to life and personal liberty, and that its suspension deprived citizens of any locus standi to challenge detention orders in courts. The majority held that judicial superintendence over executive detention was completely barred for the duration of the emergency. However, a solitary dissenting judge firmly rejected this view, holding that the sanctity of life and personal liberty is not a gift of the Constitution, but an inalienable human right that exists prior to and independent of positive law.

The dissenting opinion memorably declared that the rule of law is the antithesis of arbitrariness, and that courts must remain open to prevent lawless executive actions. Although the dissenting judge was subsequently superseded for the office of Chief Justice of India, his constitutional stance was universally vindicated when Parliament enacted the Forty-Fourth Amendment in 1978, establishing that Articles 20 and 21 can never be suspended even during an emergency. Decades later, a unanimous nine-judge Constitution Bench formally overruled the majority ruling, enshrining the dissent as the true law of the republic.

What the court decided

The Supreme Court of India allowed the state appeals by a 4 to 1 majority, setting aside the judgments of the High Courts and holding that no person had locus standi to challenge preventive detentions during the emergency; this ruling was subsequently overruled by a nine-judge Constitution Bench in 2017.

Key holdings

  • The 1976 majority held that in view of the presidential order under Article 359(1), no writ petition under Article 226 for habeas corpus was maintainable to challenge the legality or bona fides of an executive detention.
  • The historic dissent established that the right to personal liberty is an inherent common law and natural right that cannot be extinguished by executive decrees.
  • The rule of law requires that all executive action affecting the life or liberty of an individual must be backed by valid legal authority.
  • Parliament in 1978 amended Article 359(1) to make Articles 20 and 21 permanently non-derogable, and the Supreme Court in 2017 formally buried the majority decision.

Legal principles

Inviolability of the Rule of Law

The rule of law is the antithesis of arbitrariness, requiring that no person shall be deprived of life or personal liberty except by authority of valid law.

In this case: The landmark dissent held that executive authorities have no inherent power to detain citizens without statutory compliance, even during an emergency.

Non-Derogability of Inherent Natural Rights

Fundamental human rights to life and liberty exist independently of written constitutions and cannot be treated as mere gifts bestowed by the State.

In this case: The minority opinion emphasized that the right to life was not created by Article 21 and survived any temporary suspension of constitutional remedies.

Primacy of Judicial Review in Habeas Corpus

High Courts exercising constitutional writ jurisdiction possess an irreducible duty to ensure that executive detention powers conform to statutory mandates.

In this case: The nine original High Court rulings and the 2017 overruling bench affirmed that courts must never be reduced to helpless spectators before executive overreach.

The law then and now

The case was adjudicated during the 1975–1977 internal emergency under the Constitution of India and the Maintenance of Internal Security Act, 1971. In 1978, the Constitution (Forty-Fourth Amendment) Act fundamentally changed the legal framework by barring any suspension of Articles 20 and 21, and the 1976 majority decision was formally overruled by a nine-judge Constitution Bench in 2017.
