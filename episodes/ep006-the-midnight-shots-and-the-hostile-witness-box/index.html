The Midnight Shots and the Hostile Witness Box

When thirty-two eyewitnesses recant their testimony and the murder weapon vanishes, appellate courts reconstruct a fatal late-night shooting through unbroken physical evidence.

Key takeaways

  • The testimony of a hostile witness is not wiped off the record and can be relied upon to the extent it supports truth and credible corroboration.
  • Appellate courts have full jurisdiction to reverse an acquittal when the trial court's judgment is palpably unreasonable or misreads forensic evidence.
  • A murder conviction can be sustained even if the weapon is never recovered, provided that forensic and circumstantial links remain unbroken.
  • Perjury proceedings can be initiated against witnesses who deliberately retract sworn police statements during serious criminal trials.

The case in brief

In the early morning hours at an exclusive capital restaurant party, a patron demanded alcohol after the bar had closed and supplies were exhausted. When a celebrity bartender politely refused service, the patron drew a small-caliber handgun, fired an initial warning shot into the ceiling, and then discharged a second point-blank shot directly into the victim's temple. The shooter fled into the night after being confronted by restaurant proprietors, and the victim succumbed to brain trauma shortly thereafter.

During the trial, the prosecution suffered an unprecedented evidentiary breakdown when thirty-two witnesses, including direct eyewitnesses who had observed the shooting, recanted their statements and turned hostile in the witness box. A key witness introduced a novel claim that two separate shooters were present, which the defense used to construct a theory that different weapons were fired. Pointing to the hostile testimony and the failure of investigators to recover the firearm, the trial court delivered a comprehensive acquittal of all accused.

On appeal against acquittal, the high court reversed the judgment, and the Supreme Court of India affirmed the conviction. The apex court held that the Latin maxim falsus in uno, falsus in omnibus has no application in Indian courts; judges must separate the grain of truth from the chaff of falsehood in hostile testimony. The bench confirmed that the evidentiary value of a hostile witness's statements is not erased completely, and portions corroborating the prosecution remain fully admissible.

The conviction was firmly anchored in circumstantial and physical evidence that remained untainted by witness intimidation. Forensic experts matched broken glass fragments from the crime scene directly to an abandoned vehicle used by the accused, and ballistics proved that both cartridge cases were fired from a single firearm. The court held that the absence of the murder weapon is not fatal to the prosecution when the presence, motive, and subsequent conduct of the accused are established beyond reasonable doubt.

What the court decided

The Supreme Court of India dismissed the appeals, upholding the high court's reversal of acquittal and confirming the life sentence for murder.

Key holdings

  • The maxim falsus in uno, falsus in omnibus is not applicable in India, and the credible portions of a hostile witness's testimony may be relied upon.
  • An appellate court possesses full authority to reverse an acquittal if the trial court's conclusions are perverse, demonstrably unsustainable, or ignore material evidence.
  • The non-recovery of the weapon of offence is not fatal to the prosecution when ocular presence, motive, ballistics, and conduct are firmly established.
  • Subsequent conduct, including abscondence and attempting to conceal vehicles, provides strong corroborative evidence of guilt.

Legal principles

Rejection of False in One, False in All falsus in uno, falsus in omnibus

The doctrine that a witness who lies about one matter is completely untrustworthy does not apply in Indian law; courts must separate truth from falsehood in hostile testimony.

In this case: The court relied on the initial admissions and corroborating details provided by witnesses who had otherwise turned hostile to reconstruct the sequence of events.

Appellate Power to Reverse Acquittal

Appellate courts may reappraise evidence and overturn an acquittal when the trial court's reasoning is perverse or ignores vital material facts.

In this case: The court affirmed the high court's intervention because the trial court had unreasonably discarded strong circumstantial links and accepted untenable defense theories.

Sufficiency of Circumstantial Chain Without Weapon

Failure to recover the weapon of offence does not weaken the prosecution if the rest of the circumstantial and forensic evidence forms an unbroken chain.

In this case: The conviction was sustained despite the missing firearm because ballistics, matching vehicle glass, and immediate post-shooting conduct conclusively established guilt.

The law then and now

The incident took place in April 1999, and the trial and subsequent appeals were governed by the Indian Penal Code, 1860, the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, and the Indian Evidence Act, 1872. The new penal and procedural statutes (Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, and Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam) took effect on 1 July 2024 and apply prospectively.
