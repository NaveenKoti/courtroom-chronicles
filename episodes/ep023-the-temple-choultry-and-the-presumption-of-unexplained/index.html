The Temple Choultry and the Presumption of Unexplained Possession

When a female serial predator lures devotees to temple rooms and poisons them for jewelry, forensic recoveries and Section 106 burdens establish guilt.

Key takeaways

  • Failure to explain how an accused parted company with a deceased person last seen in their exclusive presence provides an incriminating missing link.
  • Unexplained possession of a victim's personal jewelry immediately following a homicide triggers a legal presumption of murder.
  • Serial criminality targeting vulnerable citizens in places of worship justifies severe sentencing restrictions including denial of remission.
  • Extensive delay in capital confirmation proceedings serves as a well-established constitutional ground for commuting death to life imprisonment.

The case in brief

In December 2007, an unsuspecting female devotee visiting suburban shrines was befriended by a woman posing as a pious spiritual guide. The accused induced the victim to participate in an auspicious multi-day ritual at an ancient rural temple complex, specifically instructing her to wear heavy gold ornaments and carry cash. Upon arriving at the pilgrimage center, the two women rented a room in a charitable choultry lodge under the pretext of conducting private religious rites.

A few hours later, the lodge manager noticed that the room had been locked from the outside with an unfamiliar external padlock while the accompanying woman had disappeared. Forcing the door open, lodge workers discovered the victim lying dead on the floor with ligature cords knotted around her neck and her gold jewelry, wristwatch, and purse stripped away. Autopsy findings confirmed that death was caused by asphyxia resulting from homicidal strangulation.

Twelve days later, police intercepted the accused at a central bus terminal, recovering the victim's distinct gold ear studs, purse, and wristwatch from her handbag alongside articles tied to other unsolved temple deaths. The designated sessions court convicted the accused of robbery and murder, imposing the death penalty. The trial judge noted that the accused operated as a serial predator targeting vulnerable women across religious shrines.

On appeal and capital reference, the High Court of Karnataka affirmed the conviction while modifying the sentence to whole-life imprisonment without remission. The division bench ruled that the prosecution had conclusively established that the accused was last seen inside the locked room with the victim, triggering a mandatory evidentiary burden under Section 106 of the Evidence Act. In the absence of any explanation, coupled with the statutory presumption under Section 114(a) arising from the recent possession of robbed ornaments, her guilt was proven beyond reasonable doubt.

What the court decided

The High Court of Karnataka dismissed the convict's appeal against conviction, declined the murder reference, and commuted the death sentence to imprisonment for the remainder of her natural life without remission.

Key holdings

  • When an accused is proven to have entered a room with the deceased and is seen leaving alone before the room is found locked, Section 106 casts the burden on the accused to explain how they parted company.
  • Under Section 114(a) of the Evidence Act, unexplained possession of articles robbed from a murdered person immediately after the crime permits the presumption that the possessor is the murderer.
  • Recovery of the victim's personal effects from the possession of the accused under Section 27 forms an unassailable link in circumstantial cases.
  • Prolonged incarceration on death row spanning seventeen years constitutes a powerful mitigating factor justifying commutation of a capital sentence to whole-life imprisonment.

Legal principles

Burden of Proof for Facts Especially Within Knowledge

When any fact is especially within the knowledge of any person, the burden of proving that fact is upon them.

In this case: The court held that having been last seen together in the locked choultry room, the accused had an obligation under Section 106 to explain what transpired inside.

Presumption from Recent Possession of Stolen Property

A court may presume that a person in possession of recently stolen goods is either the thief or received them knowing them to be stolen, extending to murder in robbery-killings.

In this case: Possession of the deceased's gold ear studs and watch without explanation justified the legal inference that the accused committed both robbery and murder.

Whole-Life Imprisonment Alternative to Capital Sentence

Appellate courts can substitute capital punishment with an explicit sentence of imprisonment for the remainder of the offender's natural life without executive remission.

In this case: The High Court commuted the death sentence but directed that the convict remain incarcerated for the remainder of her natural life.

The law then and now

The offence occurred in December 2007, and all trial and appellate proceedings were governed by the Indian Penal Code, 1860, the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, and the Indian Evidence Act, 1872. The new penal enactments (Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, and Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam) came into effect on 1 July 2024 and apply prospectively.
