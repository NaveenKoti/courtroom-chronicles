The Winter Courtyard Stove and the Conflicting Dying Declarations

When a pregnant bride burned in a freezing Delhi courtyard, the Supreme Court weighed her spontaneous cries to rescuing neighbours against a thumb-marked police statement claiming a stove accident.

Key takeaways

  • A written police dying declaration is not superior to oral dying declarations if the written record violates procedural safeguards and contradicts physical evidence.
  • Independent neighbours who intervene in a domestic emergency and bear physical injuries from the rescue are highly credible witnesses whose testimony cannot be brushed aside as over-enthusiastic.
  • Forensic inspection of household appliances—such as an unburst stove, a cold dry pan, and an unused indoor gas cylinder—can decisively refute a staged domestic accident defence.
  • Public outcry and judicial findings from bride-burning trials of the early 1980s directly led Parliament to enact specific provisions for marital cruelty, dowry death, and statutory presumptions.

The case in brief

In February 1980, a 20-year-old woman married into a Delhi trading family and moved into a ground-floor apartment. Soon after the wedding, her husband and in-laws began harassing her with demands for cash, a refrigerator, and a two-wheeler scooter, repeating the ultimatum as she entered the final month of her pregnancy in late 1980.

Shortly after 9:00 PM on 1 December 1980, neighbours heard cries for help from the flat and rushed to the entrance, where they saw the husband attempting to hold the front door shut and his brother standing with his hand on the courtyard latch. Forcing their way inside, four neighbours found the heavily pregnant woman standing engulfed in flames in the open courtyard. The rescuers tore off her burning nylon garment and smothered the fire with a gunny bag and a blanket, blistering their own hands while the husband's hands remained completely unburned.

Immediately after the flames were put out, the dying woman pointed at her mother-in-law in front of the neighbours and declared that her mother-in-law had poured kerosene on her and set her on fire—an accusation she repeated while being carried to a taxi and again to her elder sister en route to the hospital. However, at the hospital later that night, a police sub-inspector recorded a written statement—without summoning a judicial magistrate or obtaining a formal medical fitness certificate, and bearing only a blurred thumb impression despite the victim being literate—claiming her garment caught fire accidentally while heating milk on a kerosene stove.

The victim and her unborn child died early the next morning. Crime scene inspection revealed that the kerosene stove in the freezing outdoor courtyard had not burst, the milk pan beside it was completely cold and dry, and a working liquefied petroleum gas (LPG) stove was sitting inside the warm kitchen. The trial court rejected the police statement and sentenced the husband, mother-in-law, and brother-in-law to death, but in November 1983 the High Court of Delhi accepted the accidental stove theory and acquitted all three.

Hearing appeals by the Delhi Administration and a women's legal federation under Article 136 of the Constitution of India, the Supreme Court of India reversed the acquittals of the husband and mother-in-law in September 1985. Taking judicial notice of the December winter cold and the physical impossibility of the stove defence, the Court discarded the compromised police paper, accepted the corroborated oral declarations made to independent neighbours, and sentenced the husband and mother-in-law to life imprisonment while giving the brother-in-law the benefit of doubt.

What the court decided

The Supreme Court of India set aside the High Court's acquittal of the husband and mother-in-law, convicted them of murder read with common intention, and sentenced them to imprisonment for life while granting the brother-in-law the benefit of doubt.

Key holdings

  • Under Article 136 of the Constitution of India, the Supreme Court will set aside an acquittal where the High Court's appreciation of evidence is perverse and ignores glaring physical facts.
  • A written dying declaration recorded by a police officer in violation of police rules—without summoning a magistrate, without a proper medical fitness endorsement, and using a thumb impression for a literate declarant—is unreliable.
  • Spontaneous oral dying declarations made immediately after the incident to independent, disinterested rescuing neighbours are admissible and carry strong weight when corroborated by physical circumstances.
  • Courts may take judicial notice of ordinary human conduct and physical realities—such as a heavily pregnant woman in thin clothing not squatting in a freezing December courtyard to pump a slow kerosene stove when a working gas stove is inside the kitchen.
  • Because two years had elapsed since the High Court's acquittal, the Supreme Court awarded life imprisonment rather than restoring the trial court's sentence of death.

Legal principles

Dying declaration Nemo moriturus praesumitur mentiri

A statement made by a person as to the cause of their death or the circumstances of the transaction resulting in their death is admissible in evidence, grounded on the maxim that a person on the verge of death is not presumed to lie.

In this case: The court rejected the suspicious police-recorded hospital paper and relied on the victim's three spontaneous oral dying declarations made to rescuing neighbours and her sister, which were fully corroborated by the crime scene forensics.

Facts forming part of the same transaction Res gestae

Spontaneous statements or acts so closely connected with the main event in time and circumstance that they exclude the possibility of fabrication are relevant as part of the same transaction.

In this case: The victim's immediate cry to the neighbours who had just extinguished the flames—pointing to her mother-in-law as the person who poured kerosene and lit the match—carried high probative value.

Joint criminal liability by common intention

When a criminal act is done by several persons in furtherance of the common intention of all, each such person is liable for that act in the same manner as if it were done by them alone.

In this case: By holding the entrance door shut to prevent neighbours from entering while his wife burned inside the courtyard, the husband acted in concert with his mother and shared full liability for murder.

Judicial notice of natural events and human conduct

A court may take notice of indisputable facts of nature, climate, and ordinary human behaviour without requiring formal proof.

In this case: The Supreme Court took notice of the biting December night cold in Delhi and the availability of an indoor gas stove to conclude that the defence story of the pregnant victim choosing to pump an outdoor kerosene stove was impossible.

Benefit of the doubt

Where the evidence against an individual accused in a multi-accused trial lies on the borderline and does not exclude reasonable doubt as to their specific participation, they are entitled to acquittal.

In this case: While the husband's and mother-in-law's guilt was conclusively established, the court gave the brother-in-law the benefit of doubt regarding his active participation in the burning.

The law then and now

Because the offence took place in December 1980, prior to 1 July 2024, the Indian Penal Code, 1860 and the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 governed the trial and appeal; Sections 498A and 304B of the Indian Penal Code and Section 113B of the Indian Evidence Act were enacted in the 1980s following the public and legislative response to dowry burnings.
