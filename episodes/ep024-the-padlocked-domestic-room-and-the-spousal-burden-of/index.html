The Padlocked Domestic Room and the Spousal Burden of Explanation

When a mother and two infants are found strangled behind a locked door, the Supreme Court rules that an absconding husband's silence completes the circumstantial chain.

Key takeaways

  • In domestic homicides committed behind closed doors, a spouse cannot rely on mere silence when proved to be the last person present.
  • Section 106 of the Evidence Act supplies the missing link in circumstantial prosecutions when an accused conceals facts within exclusive personal knowledge.
  • A formal examination under Section 313 CrPC provides an accused the vital opportunity to present an innocent explanation, and total silence carries legal consequences.
  • Appellate courts will reverse acquittals when lower courts overlook foundational last-seen circumstances in enclosed domestic settings.

The case in brief

In February 1998, a milk delivery vendor arriving at a rented single-room residence in northwestern India was informed by the resident wife and her husband to discontinue daily supplies. The following morning, neighbors and relatives discovered that the main entrance of the house was fastened from the outside with a padlock, while the husband was missing. Three days later, concerned family members broke through the doorway to find the decomposing bodies of the wife and her two infant daughters lying on separate cots.

Autopsies conducted by medical examiners certified that all three victims had died of asphyxia resulting from ligature strangulation approximately forty-eight to seventy-two hours earlier, perfectly matching the timeline of the husband's disappearance. The husband evaded authorities for two weeks before being arrested in a distant locality. At trial, the prosecution presented undisputed testimony establishing that the husband was last seen alive with his family inside the home immediately before the padlocking of the premises.

The trial court convicted the husband of triple murder and awarded the death penalty, relying on the last-seen circumstance and custodial recoveries. On appeal, the high court overturned the conviction, holding that family members naturally reside together and that circumstantial evidence was insufficient to eliminate reasonable doubt. The state appealed the acquittal to the Supreme Court of India.

The Supreme Court reversed the acquittal, delivering an authoritative ruling on the interplay between circumstantial evidence and Section 106 of the Evidence Act. The court held that when the prosecution proves that the deceased was last seen alive in the exclusive domestic company of the accused and found dead inside a locked room, Section 106 casts an evidentiary burden on the spouse to explain how and when he parted company. Because the husband offered only a bald denial during his examination under Section 313 of the procedural code without explaining his whereabouts, his failure to furnish an explanation provided the conclusive missing link sustaining a life sentence for murder.

What the court decided

The Supreme Court of India allowed the state's criminal appeal, set aside the judgment of acquittal passed by the High Court, and convicted the respondent under Section 302 IPC, sentencing him to imprisonment for life.

Key holdings

  • When the prosecution establishes that the deceased was last seen alive in the exclusive company of the accused in an enclosed dwelling, Section 106 obliges the accused to explain what happened thereafter.
  • Failure of the accused to offer any plausible explanation regarding facts especially within his special knowledge provides an additional link completing the chain of circumstantial evidence.
  • A bald denial during examination under Section 313 of the Code of Criminal Procedure without providing an alibi or accounting for absence strengthens the prosecution case.
  • While Section 106 does not relieve the prosecution of its primary burden of proof, it operates decisively once foundational facts pointing to exclusive opportunity are established.

Legal principles

Evidentiary Burden in Domestic Last-Seen Circumstances

When an offence occurs inside a private matrimonial dwelling where the accused and deceased were last seen together, the accused must explain how they parted company.

In this case: The Supreme Court held that the husband's failure to account for his sudden disappearance after being seen with his family inside the locked house completed the chain of guilt.

Legal Effect of False Denial in Accused Examination

An outright false denial or complete refusal to explain incriminating circumstances during statutory examination can be treated as an additional corroborative link.

In this case: The court found that the husband's total silence and absence of explanation regarding his whereabouts between the lockup and his arrest completed the proof.

Relevancy of Subsequent Abscondence

Conduct of an accused immediately following a crime, such as unexplained flight and evading detection, is relevant under the law of evidence.

In this case: The husband's immediate disappearance for fourteen days following the locking of the room strongly pointed to a guilty conscience.

The law then and now

The homicides took place in February 1998, and the trial and appellate proceedings were conducted under the Indian Penal Code, 1860, the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, and the Indian Evidence Act, 1872. The new penal enactments (Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, and Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam) came into effect on 1 July 2024 and apply prospectively.
