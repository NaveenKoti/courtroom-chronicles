Key takeaways

  • Police cannot force suspects or witnesses to undergo narco-analysis, lie-detector, or brain-mapping tests.
  • Statutory medical-examination powers cover physical samples, not compelled psychological or chemical interrogation.
  • Even voluntary test statements cannot convict a person on their own without independent corroborating recoveries.

The case in brief

In April 2004, police investigating a murder in Koramangala, Bangalore subjected suspects to polygraph and brain electrical activation profile tests before obtaining a magistrate order on June 26, 2004 for involuntary narco-analysis using intravenous sodium pentothal. After the Karnataka High Court upheld the order on September 10, 2004, the suspects appealed to the Supreme Court alongside similar challenges from across the country.

On May 5, 2010, a three-judge bench unanimously struck down the involuntary use of narco-analysis, polygraph examinations, and brain-mapping techniques. The court ruled that forcing a person to undergo these tests violates Article 20(3), which protects against self-incrimination during criminal investigation, and Article 21, which safeguards mental privacy, bodily integrity, and substantive due process.

Distinguishing physical specimens such as fingerprints or DNA from mental contents, the bench held that Section 53 of the criminal procedure code does not authorize deception-detection probes. Even when an individual voluntarily consents under strict National Human Rights Commission safeguards, the test results remain inadmissible as substantive evidence, and only subsequent physical discoveries may be proved under Section 27 of the Evidence Act.

What the court decided

Appeals allowed; involuntary administration of narco-analysis, polygraph, and brain electrical activation profile tests declared unconstitutional under Articles 20(3) and 21.

Key holdings

  • Compelling an accused, suspect, or witness to undergo narco-analysis, polygraph, or brain-mapping tests amounts to testimonial compulsion barred by Article 20(3) even at the investigative stage.
  • Unlike physical samples such as fingerprints, blood, or handwriting, deception-detection techniques extract personal knowledge from the mind and fall outside medical examination under Section 53 of the Code of Criminal Procedure.
  • Intruding into a person's cognitive autonomy without consent violates the right to mental privacy, bodily integrity, and protection against cruel, inhuman, or degrading treatment under Article 21.
  • Voluntary tests are permitted only under strict safeguards—including consent recorded before a Judicial Magistrate with legal counsel—and test results themselves are never substantive evidence, though physical recoveries made afterward may be admitted under Section 27 of the Evidence Act.

Legal principles

Protection Against Testimonial Compulsion

The constitutional bar on self-incrimination protects an individual from being forced to impart personal knowledge through words or physiological responses during police investigation.

In this case: The court drew a firm boundary between collecting physical specimens for comparison and extracting hidden mental contents through drugs or neural monitoring.

Mental Privacy and Substantive Due Process

Article 21 protects an inner sphere of cognitive privacy and bodily autonomy against arbitrary or degrading state intrusion.

In this case: Injecting barbiturates or wiring a suspect's brain without free consent was held to be an unconstitutional invasion of personal liberty.

Inadmissibility of Coerced Scientific Techniques

Even where an accused consents to a deception-detection procedure under judicial supervision, the raw output cannot serve as standalone proof of guilt.

In this case: The bench mandated adherence to National Human Rights Commission guidelines and limited evidentiary use strictly to independent physical discoveries under Section 27.
