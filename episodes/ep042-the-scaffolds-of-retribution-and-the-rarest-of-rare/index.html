Key takeaways

  • Life imprisonment is the standard statutory sentence for murder, and execution is strictly exceptional.
  • The judicial evaluation of sentence must focus as much on the character and rehabilitation potential of the criminal as on the brutality of the crime.
  • Statutory safeguards and confirmation requirements prevent arbitrary or capricious imposition of capital penalties.

The case in brief

Following the violent killing of three sleeping relatives in a village courtyard, an accused individual who had previously completed a life sentence for wife murder was convicted of capital murder and sentenced to execution. On appeal, differing appellate perspectives on capital punishment prompted a reference to a five-judge Constitution Bench to re-evaluate whether death penalty provisions violated fundamental guarantees of equality, freedom, and life.

The petitioner contended that capital punishment constitutes an irreversible and degrading deprivation of human dignity lacking empirical proof of deterrence, arguing that statutory requirements to record special reasons conferred arbitrary and untrammeled discretion on sentencing judges. The State countered that personal liberties are conditioned on reasonable penal sanctions, asserting that judicial sentencing discretion guided by statutory policy complies with constitutional due process.

The Supreme Court affirmed the constitutional validity of capital punishment, ruling that the deprivation of life under a fair, just, and reasonable statutory procedure does not violate fundamental rights. The bench formulated the enduring rarest of rare doctrine, mandating that life imprisonment is the rule and capital execution an exceptional penalty permissible only when aggravating circumstances overwhelmingly surpass mitigating factors and the alternative option is unquestionably foreclosed.

What the court decided

Constitutional validity of death penalty for murder under Section 302 IPC and sentencing procedure under Section 354(3) CrPC upheld by four-to-one majority; individual appeal remitted for sentence reconsideration.

Key holdings

  • The provision of death penalty as an alternative punishment for murder under Section 302 IPC does not violate Articles 14, 19, or 21 of the Constitution.
  • Section 354(3) of the Code of Criminal Procedure establishes life imprisonment as the general rule and capital punishment as an exceptional departure.
  • Sentencing judges must evaluate both the nature of the crime and the circumstances of the criminal, balancing aggravating and mitigating factors.
  • Capital punishment can be imposed only in the rarest of rare cases where the alternative option of life imprisonment is unquestionably foreclosed.

Legal principles

Rarest of Rare Doctrine

Capital punishment should be inflicted only in exceptional cases of extreme depravity where the criminal demonstrates zero potential for reform and society's conscience demands ultimate retribution.

In this case: The court held that judges must reserve death sentences for extreme culpability where all alternative avenues of penological rehabilitation are entirely closed.

Balance Sheet of Aggravating and Mitigating Factors

Sentencing requires an individualized balancing of circumstances surrounding both the crime and the offender before imposing capital sanctions.

In this case: The court mandated that judicial discretion must consider mitigating elements relating to the offender, including age, background, and probability of reform, alongside the gravity of the crime.

Constitutional Due Process in Sentencing

A statutory procedure depriving an individual of life must be just, fair, and reasonable, incorporating pre-sentence hearings and appellate confirmation safeguards.

In this case: The mandatory pre-sentence hearing under Section 235(2) and High Court confirmation under Section 366 were held to provide robust protection against arbitrary execution.

The law then and now

Because the offense occurred in July 1977, the substantive trial and sentencing were governed by the Indian Penal Code, 1860, and the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, prior to the transition to the Bharatiya Sanhitas in July 2024.
