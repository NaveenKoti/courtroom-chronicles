When a toxic chemical gas plume blankets an urban metropolis, the Supreme Court formulates the doctrine of absolute liability with zero common law exceptions.

Key takeaways

  • Hazardous commercial enterprises are strictly liable as insurers of community safety, regardless of whether reasonable operational care was exercised.
  • Common law exceptions like acts of strangers or unforeseen events cannot excuse corporations from compensating victims of chemical escapes.
  • The right to life under Article 21 includes the fundamental entitlement to reside in a clean, safe, and unpolluted environment.
  • The Supreme Court possesses expansive remedial authority under Article 32 to devise innovative remedies and award direct financial compensation.

The case in brief

In December 1985, an urban capital was plunged into panic when a massive cloud of toxic oleum gas escaped from a chemical fertilizer plant situated within a densely populated industrial zone. The corrosive acidic fumes drifted into surrounding neighborhoods and nearby judicial courts, causing widespread respiratory trauma and the death of a practicing advocate. The magistrate promptly invoked nuisance removal powers under Section 133 of the criminal procedure code to seal the industrial complex.

A public interest environmental lawyer had previously filed a writ petition before the Supreme Court seeking the relocation of hazardous chemical industries away from congested urban settlements. Following the toxic release, legal aid organizations and bar associations filed applications under Article 32 seeking monetary compensation for affected victims. In response, the corporate management contended that private commercial companies were outside the constitutional discipline of Article 21, and that liability was governed by nineteenth-century English common law strict liability rules permitting defense exceptions such as an act of a stranger or statutory authorization.

A five-judge Constitution Bench rejected the application of obsolete foreign precedents, boldly formulating an indigenous standard of absolute liability for modern industrial hazards. The court ruled that where an enterprise engages in an inherently dangerous activity for private profit, it owes an absolute and non-delegable duty to the community to ensure no harm results. Under this doctrine, the enterprise cannot escape liability by claiming that it exercised all reasonable care or that the escape occurred without operational negligence.

The Supreme Court also held that its remedial powers under Article 32 are not merely preventive or injunctive, but extend to awarding compensatory damages for patent violations of the fundamental right to life under Article 21. Furthermore, the bench laid down the deep pocket principle, establishing that the measure of compensation must be correlated with the magnitude and financial capacity of the enterprise to ensure a genuine deterrent effect. The landmark ruling revolutionized environmental jurisprudence across the global south.

What the court decided

The Supreme Court of India established the doctrine of absolute liability for hazardous industries, directed the local legal aid board to file victim compensation claims before the district judge, and affirmed its remedial jurisdiction under Article 32 to grant damages for violations of Article 21.

Key holdings

  • An enterprise engaged in a hazardous or inherently dangerous industry owes an absolute and non-delegable duty to ensure that no harm results to anyone.
  • The rule of absolute liability is not subject to any of the traditional exceptions applicable to the common law rule of strict liability.
  • Under the deep pocket principle, the measure of compensation must be correlated to the magnitude and financial capacity of the enterprise to ensure adequate deterrence.
  • Article 32 confers an affirmative constitutional duty upon the Supreme Court to forge new remedies, including the award of monetary compensation for gross breaches of fundamental rights.

Legal principles

Doctrine of Absolute Liability

An enterprise engaged in an inherently dangerous activity is strictly and absolutely liable for all harm caused by an escape, with zero defenses or exceptions.

In this case: The court discarded nineteenth-century foreign common law exceptions, holding the chemical manufacturer absolutely answerable for all injuries caused by the toxic vapor escape.

Deep Pocket Principle in Tortious Compensation

The quantum of damages payable for environmental and industrial disasters must be proportional to the commercial wealth and operational size of the offending corporation.

In this case: The court held that larger and more prosperous corporations must pay higher damages to maintain meaningful economic deterrence against industrial negligence.

Inherent Compensatory Powers under Constitutional Writ Jurisdiction

The power of constitutional courts to issue directions for the enforcement of fundamental rights includes the remedial power to award monetary compensation for human rights violations.

In this case: The bench established that Article 32 empowers the apex court to award financial damages for gross and patent violations of the right to life under Article 21.

The law then and now

The proceedings were adjudicated under the Constitution of India and the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973. In contemporary practice, public nuisance powers are codified under Section 152 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, while the doctrine of absolute liability remains foundational constitutional tort law.
