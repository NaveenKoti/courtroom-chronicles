Thirty-Three Bullets and the Circumstantial Proof of Conspiracy

A landmark constitutional ruling established that capital conspiracy cannot rest on suspicion or innocent association without proof of an agreement.

Key takeaways

  • Circumstantial evidence in conspiracy cases must unerringly point to an agreement and exclude every hypothesis of innocence.
  • Police custody must be strictly accounted for, and uncorroborated arrests with convenient recoveries will be rejected by appellate courts.
  • Security constraints may dictate the physical venue of a trial without depriving the proceeding of its public and open character.

The case in brief

When the elected head of government was assassinated on the garden pathway of her official residence by two members of her personal security detail, investigators sought to prove that the attack was part of an extensive criminal conspiracy. The prosecution charged an injured surviving guard, a subordinate police officer who had allegedly displayed resentment, and an administrative government clerk related by marriage to one of the deceased assailants.

In a special trial conducted within the confines of a central prison complex, all three accused were convicted of murder and criminal conspiracy and sentenced to capital punishment. On final appeal, the Supreme Court rigorously scrutinized the circumstantial evidence, examining whether the venue of trial infringed the requirement of an open and public trial, and whether isolated acts of religious devotion or private meetings satisfied the statutory threshold for conspiracy under evidentiary rules.

The Supreme Court rendered a divided outcome, unanimously acquitting the subordinate police officer after finding the alleged seizure of an incriminating memorandum fabricated and destitute of independent corroboration. However, the court confirmed the convictions and death penalties of the surviving guard and the administrative clerk, ruling that the direct ballistic testimony and the clerk's continuous clandestine conduct with the principal assailant established guilt beyond reasonable doubt.

What the court decided

Appeals of the surviving guard and the clerk dismissed with death sentences confirmed; appeal of the accused police officer allowed with complete acquittal.

Key holdings

  • Holding a criminal trial in an office block of a prison does not violate the open court requirement if public and press access is permitted subject to reasonable security regulation.
  • Section 10 of the Evidence Act requires prima facie proof of a shared unlawful agreement before acts or statements of one person can be used against another.
  • Expressions of political or religious grief and innocuous associations cannot substitute for concrete proof of an agreement to commit murder.
  • The assassination of an elected national leader by duty-bound security personnel constitutes an exceptional category warranting capital punishment.

Legal principles

Requirement of Prima Facie Agreement in Conspiracy

Acts or statements of a co-conspirator become admissible against another only after reasonable grounds independently establish that they conspired together.

In this case: The court held that evidentiary provisions could not be invoked against the acquitted police officer because the prosecution failed to prove an initial agreement to commit assassination.

Open Court Principle in Exceptional Venues

Any venue where a criminal court sits is deemed an open court by law, provided the public and press are afforded reasonable access within capacity limits.

In this case: Holding the proceedings in a prison administration block was upheld as a valid public trial because accredited journalists and visitors attended subject to security protocols.

Rarest of Rare Doctrine in Duty Betrayal

Extreme statutory penalties are reserved for egregious offenses where duty-bound protectors murder the protected person without mitigating factors.

In this case: The capital sentence was confirmed because armed security guards deliberately turned their service weapons against the unarmed official they were sworn to protect.

The law then and now

Because the incident occurred in October 1984, the trial and convictions were governed by the Indian Penal Code, 1860, the Indian Evidence Act, 1872, and the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, prior to the enactment of the new criminal codes on July 1, 2024.
