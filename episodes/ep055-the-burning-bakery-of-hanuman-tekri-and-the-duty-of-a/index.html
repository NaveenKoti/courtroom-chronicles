The Burning Bakery of Hanuman Tekri and the Duty of a Trial Judge

After thirty-seven witnesses turned hostile in a massacre trial, the Supreme Court ordered an unprecedented out-of-state retrial and jailed a perjuring witness.

Key takeaways

  • Trial judges must actively use statutory questioning and witness-summoning powers when intimidation threatens to derail justice.
  • Witness protection is a constitutional necessity under Article 21, not an optional administrative courtesy.
  • Deliberate perjury and paid reversals by a witness undermine the justice system and attract criminal contempt sanctions.

The case in brief

On the night of March 1, 2002, an armed mob encircled and torched a two-story bakery in the Hanuman Tekri neighborhood of Vadodara, killing fourteen people trapped inside. A nineteen-year-old survivor named twenty-one attackers in a hospital police statement, but when the fast-track trial opened in May 2003, thirty-seven of forty-three prosecution witnesses retracted their statements and all twenty-one accused were acquitted.

After fleeing to Mumbai, the star witness alleged that death threats from local political figures had forced her courtroom retraction, prompting the National Human Rights Commission and the survivor to move the Supreme Court. On April 12, 2004, the Supreme Court held that the trial was a sham because the prosecutor and trial judge had acted as passive spectators while witnesses were intimidated, and invoked Article 142 and Section 406 to transfer the retrial out of Gujarat to Mumbai.

During the Mumbai retrial, however, the same star witness reversed her story a second time and claimed she had been coerced by activists, leading the Supreme Court to order a forensic financial inquiry by its Registrar General. When unexplained bank deposits exposed deliberate, paid perjury, the court sentenced her to one year of simple imprisonment for contempt on March 8, 2006, while the Mumbai special court relied on steadfast eyewitnesses and forensic evidence to convict nine killers.

What the court decided

Acquittals quashed and de novo retrial transferred to Maharashtra (2004); star witness later convicted of criminal contempt and sentenced to one year of simple imprisonment and a fifty-thousand-rupee fine (2006).

Key holdings

  • A criminal trial judge is not a mere tape recorder or mute spectator; under Section 165 of the Indian Evidence Act and Section 311 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, the judge has an active duty to question and summon witnesses to discover the truth.
  • The fundamental right to a fair trial under Article 21 belongs equally to victims, witnesses, and society, requiring the State to protect witnesses from intimidation.
  • Under Article 142 and Section 406, the Supreme Court may transfer a criminal trial outside a state when local intimidation makes an impartial trial impossible.
  • The testimony of a hostile witness is not automatically erased; courts may accept corroborated portions, while witnesses who accept inducements to lie under oath face summary punishment for contempt.

Legal principles

Active Judicial Truth-Seeking

A trial judge has an affirmative statutory duty to intervene, question witnesses, and summon essential evidence rather than passively recording coerced retractions.

In this case: The Supreme Court held that the fast-track judge abdicated his duty under Section 165 of the Evidence Act and Section 311 of the procedural code when dozens of witnesses suddenly recanted.

Triangulation of Fair Trial Rights

Fairness in criminal justice requires balancing the rights of the accused, the victim, and the broader public interest.

In this case: Because witness intimidation destroyed the integrity of the Vadodara trial, the court declared the acquittals a nullity and ordered a fresh trial in another state.

Separating Grain from Chaff in Hostile Testimony

When a witness turns hostile, the court need not discard their entire testimony if parts of it are reliable and corroborated by independent evidence.

In this case: During the Mumbai retrial, the special court convicted nine perpetrators using steadfast witnesses alongside forensic and medical records despite the star witness's second reversal.

The law then and now

Because the arson attack and trials took place before July 1, 2024, the proceedings were governed by the Indian Penal Code, 1860, the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, and the Indian Evidence Act, 1872.
