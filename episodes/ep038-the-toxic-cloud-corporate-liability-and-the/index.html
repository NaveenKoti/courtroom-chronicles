A landmark constitutional ruling established that court-assisted mass disaster settlements cannot extinguish criminal liability for corporate homicide.

Key takeaways

  • Civil compromise agreements cannot buy immunity from criminal prosecution for fatal industrial negligence.
  • Mass disaster victims are entitled to lifelong medical surveillance and specialized treatment facilities funded by enterprise liability.
  • Sovereign litigation on behalf of citizens imposes a fiduciary duty on the state to guarantee adequate redress.

The case in brief

When a catastrophic release of lethal chemical vapor from an industrial storage facility devastated an entire city, claiming thousands of lives, the national government assumed exclusive statutory representation of the victims under the doctrine of parens patriae. Following extensive transnational jurisdictional disputes in foreign courts, litigation for damages commenced before domestic tribunals.

In early 1989, while hearing interlocutory appeals concerning interim compensation, the Supreme Court recorded a comprehensive court-assisted financial settlement of four hundred and seventy million dollars that simultaneously quashed all pending and future criminal proceedings. Victim organizations and legal activists challenged the settlement on review, contending that extinguishment of criminal prosecutions without hearing the victims violated natural justice and was contrary to public policy.

The Constitution Bench upheld the civil compensation fund, determining that protracted litigation across decades would deprive impoverished survivors of essential survival relief, with the welfare state remaining obligated to cover any financial shortfall. However, the court decisively set aside the termination of criminal cases, ruling that judicial settlement of civil claims cannot confer criminal immunity or stifle the prosecution of serious non-compoundable corporate offenses.

What the court decided

Civil compensation settlement upheld with state shortfall guarantee; quashing of criminal prosecutions set aside and criminal proceedings revived.

Key holdings

  • Plenary constitutional powers under Article 142 allow the apex court to withdraw and dispose of connected suits to do complete justice.
  • Granting blanket immunity from criminal prosecution is an impermissible judicial act that usurps legislative authority.
  • The settlement of pre-existing civil tort liabilities does not constitute unlawful stifling of prosecution when entered into by the sovereign representing victims.
  • The state, as a constitutional welfare entity, remains under an enforceable duty to satisfy any deficiency if the settlement fund proves insufficient.

Legal principles

Parens Patriae Doctrine in Mass Disasters

The sovereign state is empowered to represent and protect the rights of vulnerable citizens who are incapacitated from pursuing complex litigation individually.

In this case: The central government was held competent to exclusively represent hundreds of thousands of toxic gas victims in domestic and transnational courts.

Inadmissibility of Judicial Criminal Immunity

Courts cannot grant blanket immunity against criminal prosecution for serious non-compoundable offenses as a term of civil settlement.

In this case: The Supreme Court revoked its earlier termination of criminal charges against corporate directors and managers, affirming that criminal law must take its course.

Welfare State Guarantee of Compensation

When the state assumes exclusive control of citizens' tort claims, it must indemnify the victims if the recovered compensation pool falls short.

In this case: The court held that the Union of India was obligated to make good any deficiency should the monetary fund prove inadequate for all valid medical claims.
